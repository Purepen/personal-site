Forecasting Tanzania's Air Quality

My first machine learning project: an ARIMA model that learned Dar es Salaam's PM2.5 patterns well enough to cut prediction error by more than 80% against a naive baseline.

Time series ARIMA statsmodels EDA

This was my first machine learning project, and I chose to start with time series on purpose. Environmental factors shape everyday health in ways that are easy to overlook, so I picked air quality: a dataset of PM2.5 sensor readings from Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, spanning December 2017 through May 2018, sourced from africaopendata.org.

Working from a sensor's raw readings, I ran the data through cleaning, exploratory analysis of how the sensor's location and reading distribution behaved, and a grid search over ARIMA's (p, q) hyperparameters, evaluated each combination with training MAE, then validated the winner with proper walk-forward validation, retraining the model at each step and only ever predicting one step past what it had already seen. That discipline matters: it's the difference between a model that looks good on paper and one that would actually hold up making tomorrow's prediction with only today's data.

Why the baseline matters as much as the model

Before tuning anything, I set a baseline: predict the training mean for every point, which scored a mean absolute error of 19.66. That number is the whole point of building a baseline in the first place, it's not a bar to be proud of clearing, it's a sanity check that keeps you honest about whether the model is actually learning something or just memorizing an average. After the grid search and walk-forward validation, the tuned ARIMA model brought that down to 3.64, an error small enough to trust for the kind of forecast that could actually inform someone's day.

What the data itself is saying

Dar es Salaam's present-day air quality sits in the 0 to 50 PM2.5 range, which reads as good on the standard scale, and the gap between the naive baseline and the tuned model's error shows there's real, learnable structure in how the pollution level moves over time, not just noise. That's a useful thing to know both for the model and for anyone trying to plan around it.
